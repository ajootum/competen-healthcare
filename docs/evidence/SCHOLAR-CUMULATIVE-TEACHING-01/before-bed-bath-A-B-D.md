OVERVIEW: These passages cover skin, hygiene and eye care for critically ill patients — areas where careful nursing prevents serious complications such as corneal ulceration, cardiovascular instability and skin tears [S5][S6][S3].

KEY POINTS:
- A daily bed-bath is standard, with frequency increased for patients who are sweating, incontinent, bleeding or have leaking wounds [S2].
- Pain should be anticipated before a bed-bath, as being moved is painful for many critically ill patients, and prophylactic pain relief may be appropriate [S2].
- Timing matters: the bed-bath should be planned around medical rounds, chest X-ray requirements and family visits, and privacy must be ensured [S3].
- Personal hygiene also includes washing hair, shaving, managing cerumen in ears, and care of finger and toe nails [S3].
- Patients requiring total care are at risk of skin tears, so careful handling to reduce friction and shear during repositioning is important [S4].
- Eye care is critical because improperly cared-for eyes can cause many hours of unnecessary discomfort, and protective mechanisms such as blinking and tears can be impaired in critically ill patients [S5].
- Eye assessment is done every 12 hours, using a grading system based on how much of the eye is exposed [S7][S8].
- Grade 1 requires ointment applied to the eye surface every 4 hours; Grade 2 requires taping the eyelids horizontally with micropore, ensuring lashes face outwards [S9][S10].

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING:
- Why might a longer bed-bath become a concern for a critically ill patient, and what does your passage say can happen? [S3]
- How does the grading system help guide eye care decisions, and what distinguishes one grade from another? [S8]
[BEYOND THE NOTES] In critical care, the body's normal protective reflexes are often reduced or absent, making routine hygiene tasks carry greater clinical risk than in general ward settings. Anticipating discomfort, planning timing carefully, and using a systematic assessment framework are general principles that underpin safe care in this environment.

Here's how the eye care part of your notes fits together.

Start with why eyes matter at all: the eyes are one of the most sensitive parts of the human body, and poor care can leave critical care patients in many hours of unnecessary discomfort [S5]. Normally the body protects itself: blinking spreads tears that moisten the eyes and have antimicrobial properties [S5]. So the underlying principle is that when the eyes cannot close, the tear film evaporates more quickly, removing that natural protection [S5].

Now connect that to the critically ill patient. The blink response may be slowed or absent because of sedatives, muscle relaxants or muscular disease such as Guillen-Barré syndrome [S6]. Lose the blink, lose the protective tear film, and you open the door to the complications listed: keratopathy, corneal ulceration, conjunctivitis, subconjunctival haemorrhage, and trauma from IV lines, ventilation tubes and NGTs [S6]. Ventilation adds its own risk — patients on PPV can develop conjunctival edema (Chemosis or "Ventilator eye"), and CPAP may lead to drying [S7].

This is why assessment grades exposure rather than guessing. Grade 0 is lids completely closed; Grade 1 is any conjunctival exposure with no cornea showing; Grade 2 is any corneal exposure [S8]. The grade drives the care: Grade 1 gets the eye bathed, examined and ointment applied every 4 hours [S9]; Grade 2, where cornea is exposed, moves to taping the eyelids with micropore [S10]. The logic throughout is to restore the moisture and protection the blink would normally give.
[BEYOND THE NOTES] Matching the level of intervention to the degree of risk is a general theme in nursing care: you assess, grade severity, and escalate only as far as needed, then reassess regularly.
