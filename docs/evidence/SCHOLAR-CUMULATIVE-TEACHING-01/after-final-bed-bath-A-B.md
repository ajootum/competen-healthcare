OVERVIEW: This lesson covers the full chain of essential nursing care for critically ill patients, from skin and tissue assessment through bed bathing, skin tear prevention, and into eye care — including how to assess the eye and what to do for a patient at Grade 1. [S5] It matters because the eyes are one of the most sensitive parts of the human body, and improperly cared-for eyes can leave patients in unnecessary discomfort for many hours. [S5]

UNDERSTANDING:
Skin and tissue assessment is where care begins. [S1] Your notes name it as a foundation, though the detail of what it involves is not in these passages. It sets the scene for everything that follows: once you have assessed the skin, you act on what you find.

One of those actions is the bed bath. A daily bed bath with intermittent washes of the face and hands is standard care, and you increase the frequency when a patient is sweating, incontinent, bleeding, or has leaking wounds. [S2] Because being moved is painful for many critically ill patients, it may be appropriate to give prophylactic pain relief before a bed bath. [S2] Timing matters too: planning ahead around medical rounds, chest X-ray requirements, and family visits protects the patient's privacy and minimises disruption. [S3] There is also a physical risk to consider — the length of time spent providing a bed bath can cause heat loss, and vasoconstriction from that heat loss may lead to cardiovascular instability. [S3] Personal hygiene within this care includes washing hair as necessary, shaving the patient, managing cerumen in the ears, and caring for finger and toe nails. [S3]

Alongside bathing, patients requiring total care must be handled carefully during repositioning to reduce friction and shear, which is how skin tears are prevented. [S4] This connects directly back to skin and tissue assessment: what you assess, you then protect.

Eye care grows naturally from this same principle of protection. The eyes are one of the most sensitive parts of the human body, and physiological mechanisms — blinking and tears — normally moisten the eyes and provide antimicrobial properties. [S5] In critically ill patients these mechanisms can fail. The blink response may be slowed or absent due to sedatives, muscle relaxants, or muscular disease such as Guillain-Barré syndrome. [S6] When eyes are unable to close, the tear film evaporates more quickly. [S5] Even simple bedside procedures such as turning on lights at night or assessing pupil reactions can be uncomfortable. [S5] The complications that follow from unprotected eyes include keratopathy, corneal ulceration, viral or bacterial conjunctivitis, subconjunctival haemorrhage, and trauma from IV lines, ventilation tubes, or NGTs. [S6]

Because the risk is real and serious, eye care assessment is performed every twelve hours for at-risk patients, whether conscious or unconscious. [S7] Patients receiving PPV may develop conjunctival oedema — known as chemosis or "ventilator eye" — and CPAP may lead to drying. [S7] The assessment identifies the at-risk patient by looking for incomplete closing of the eyelids, called lagophthalmos, and assigns a grade. [S8] At Grade 0, the lids are completely closed. At Grade 1, there is any conjunctival exposure — the white of the eye is visible — but no cornea is exposed. At Grade 2, there is any corneal exposure, even a tiny amount. [S8]

Grade 1 triggers essential care. You begin by bathing the eye in warm water with gauze, then examine the eye for redness, corneal opacity or dullness, and swelling or chemosis. [S9] Once examined, ointment — your notes name Lacrilube and VitA-POS — is applied to the eye surface every four hours. [S9]

Grade 2 requires a further step: taping the eyelids. The eyelashes must be facing outwards, and the tape is applied horizontally using micropore. [S10]

- The blink response may be slowed or absent due to sedatives, muscle relaxants, or muscular disease such as Guillain-Barré syndrome. [S6]
- Complications of poor eye care include keratopathy, corneal ulceration, viral or bacterial conjunctivitis, subconjunctival haemorrhage, and trauma. [S6]
- Eye care assessment is performed every twelve hours, including for patients on PPV who may develop chemosis, and those on CPAP who may experience drying. [S7]

The passages set out the sequence: bathe the eye in warm water with gauze, then look at the eye, then apply ointment to the eye surface every 4 hours [S9]. Bathing first means you are cleaning and moistening the surface before you treat it, which connects back to the whole purpose of eye care — replacing the physiological protection that blinking and tears would normally give when the eyes cannot close [S5][S9].

When you examine the eye, the notes name what to look for: redness, corneal opacity or dullness, and swelling or chemosis [S9]. These matter because they overlap with the complications eye care exists to prevent, so the examination is both a check and an early warning [S6][S9].

Grade 2 is corneal exposure, even a tiny amount, and here the passages add a further action: taping the eyelids [S8][S10]. The method is specific — ensure the eyelashes face outwards, then tape horizontally with micropore [S10]. So the grades are not just labels: each names a different depth of exposure and leads to a matched response — ointment for conjunctival exposure, taping once the cornea is exposed [S8][S9][S10].

- Grade 2 care is taping the eyelids, with eyelashes facing outwards, taped horizontally with micropore [S10].
