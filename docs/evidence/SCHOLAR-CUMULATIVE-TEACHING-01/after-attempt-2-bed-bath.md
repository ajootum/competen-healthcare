OVERVIEW: This lesson covers the full sequence of skin and eye care for the critically ill patient, from daily hygiene and skin protection through to assessing and managing eye exposure. It matters because poor eye care can leave a patient in unnecessary discomfort and cause serious complications [S5], while poor skin and hygiene care can cause further harm to an already vulnerable person [S2].

UNDERSTANDING:
Skin and tissue assessment forms the starting point for all care that follows [S1]. Before any hands-on care begins, knowing the condition of the skin guides every decision about how carefully to handle the patient and how frequently to intervene.

The bed bath is the main vehicle for that hands-on care. A daily bed bath with intermittent washes of the face and hands is standard, but the frequency should increase for patients who are sweating, incontinent, bleeding or have leaking wounds [S2]. Because being moved is painful for many critically ill patients, it may be appropriate to give prophylactic pain relief before a bed bath [S2]. Timing also matters: planning ahead around medical rounds, chest X-ray requirements and family visits is important, and privacy must be ensured for the patient [S3]. The length of time spent giving the bath is itself a risk — heat loss can cause vasoconstriction, which may lead to cardiovascular instability [S3]. Personal hygiene within this care includes washing hair as necessary, shaving the patient, management of cerumen in the ears, and care of finger and toe nails [S3].

For patients requiring total care, handling carefully to reduce friction and shear during repositioning is essential to protect against skin tears [S4].

From skin care, the lesson moves to a part of the body that needs its own specific attention: the eyes. The eyes are one of the most sensitive parts of the human body, and if they are improperly cared for, critical care patients may spend many hours in unnecessary discomfort [S5]. Under usual conditions, blinking and tears moisten the eyes and provide antimicrobial protection; when the eyes are unable to close, the tear film evaporates more quickly, removing both of those protections [S5]. Even simple bedside procedures — turning on lights at night or assessing pupil reactions — can be uncomfortable [S5].

In critical illness, the blink response may be slowed or absent due to sedatives, muscle relaxants, or conditions such as Guillain-Barré syndrome [S6]. This opens the door to a range of complications: keratopathy, corneal ulceration, viral or bacterial conjunctivitis, subconjunctival haemorrhage, and trauma from IV lines, ventilation tubes or NGTs [S6]. Patients receiving positive pressure ventilation may develop conjunctival oedema — known as chemosis or "ventilator eye" — and CPAP may lead to drying [S7].

Because of these risks, eye care assessment is carried out every twelve hours in at-risk patients, whether conscious or unconscious [S7]. The key is identifying the at-risk patient by looking for incomplete eyelid closing, called lagophthalmos [S8]. The assessment grades exposure: Grade 0 means lids are completely closed; Grade 1 means there is any conjunctival exposure (the white of the eye) but no cornea is exposed; Grade 2 means there is any corneal exposure, even a tiny amount [S8].

The grade then directly determines the care given. For Grade 1, the eye is first bathed in warm water with gauze, then examined for redness, corneal opacity or dullness, and swelling or chemosis [S9]. Ointment — such as Lacrillube or VitA-POS — is then applied to the eye surface every four hours [S9].

For Grade 2, where the cornea itself is exposed, the eyelids are taped. The eyelashes must be facing outwards, and the tape is applied horizontally using micropore [S10].

The eye care assessment is done every 12 hours for at-risk patients, whether conscious or unconscious [S7]. Being awake does not protect the eye, because risk comes from the eye's exposure, not the patient's alertness [S7]. Positive pressure ventilation can cause conjunctival oedema, called chemosis or "ventilator eye," and CPAP can lead to drying, so the kind of respiratory support a patient receives shapes the risk [S7].

The assessment then grades the at-risk patient by how far the eyelids close, a problem the notes name lagophthalmos, meaning incomplete closing [S8]. Grade 0 is lids completely closed [S8]. Grade 1 is any conjunctival exposure, the white of the eye, with no cornea showing [S8]. Grade 2 is any corneal exposure, even a tiny amount [S8]. The grading rises with how much of the eye is uncovered, and it matters because the cornea is what Grade 2 protects, which is why even a tiny amount of corneal exposure moves the grade up [S8].

This grading is what connects assessment to action. So the same assessment that measures exposure selects the care, which you then re-check at the next 12-hour assessment [S7][S8].
