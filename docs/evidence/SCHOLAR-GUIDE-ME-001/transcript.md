Reflexes carry out bodily functions and responses to external stimuli [S3]. They represent neurological maturation, and most develop before birth [S5]. Meanwhile, the newborn's own movements are random, diffuse and uncoordinated [S3].

SECTION: science | scholar | The science
A reflex is a fixed, automatic response wired through the spinal cord and lower brain, so it does not need the thinking part of the brain to decide anything. This is why it can be ready before birth: the pathway is built in, not learned.

Voluntary movement is different. It depends on the upper brain sending controlled signals to muscles, and those pathways are still immature in the newborn. Until they mature, the baby's own movements stay broad and unguided, while the built-in reflexes already work smoothly.

## Just explain it

```text
FOCUS QUESTION: Why sucking is smooth while arm movements are not

SECTION: principle | notes | The principle
A newborn's movements are random, diffuse and uncoordinated [S3]. Yet sucking belongs among the reflexes, the group that carries out bodily functions and responses to external stimuli [S3]. Rooting and sucking are described as nutritive reflexes [S6].

SECTION: science | scholar | Why a reflex runs so smoothly
A reflex is a fixed response that is already wired through the lower, more mature parts of the nervous system. Because its pathway is built in, the same clean movement happens every time the right stimulus arrives, without the baby having to plan or correct it. Sucking rides on this ready-made wiring, so it looks coordinated from the start.

SECTION: science | scholar | Why the arms are still clumsy
Voluntary arm movement is different: it depends on the higher, thinking parts of the brain and on fine control pathways that mature later. These pathways are still forming, so the signals reaching the muscles are not yet refined. The result is movement that spreads out and overshoots, which is why it looks random and uncoordinated. In short, the parts of the nervous system that drive sucking are ready earlier than the parts that drive skilled reaching.

SECTION: misconception | scholar | Where students go wrong
Students often assume that because sucking looks controlled, the whole nervous system must already be well organised. The opposite is closer to the truth: smooth sucking is a sign of a reflex, not of practised skill. Reaching looks messy precisely because it is a voluntary skill the baby has not yet trained up.

SECTION: memory | scholar | A cue to hold the contrast
Picture the reflex as a track already laid down, so the train runs straight every time. Voluntary arm movement is a track still being built, so the train wanders until the rails are finished.
```

FOCUS QUESTION: Why crying is the newborn's only emotional language

SECTION: principle | notes | How emotion is shown at this stage
The newborn infant expresses emotion just through cry, for hunger, pain or discomfort sensation [S9]. Within a few days, the infant begins to interact with caregivers through eye contact and spontaneous or imitative facial gestures, and these interactions strengthen emotional ties with caregivers [S10].

SECTION: science | scholar | Why crying comes first
Crying is driven by the same lower, automatic parts of the brain that run reflexes, so it is ready at birth without any learning. Signalling distress by sound costs the infant nothing and reaches a caregiver even in the dark or from another room, which is why it appears before any finer way of communicating.

Eye contact and facial gestures, by contrast, need the infant to take in another person and respond back. This is a shared, back-and-forth exchange, so it develops as the infant starts to attend to the people around it.

SECTION: memory | scholar | A way to hold it
Think of crying as the alarm that comes already switched on, and eye contact and facial gestures as the first conversation the infant learns to join.
